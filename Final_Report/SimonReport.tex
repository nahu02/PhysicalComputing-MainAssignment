\subsubsection{3D Prototyping}

\paragraph{Embedded Button Caps}
The introduction of these parts played an important role in arriving at the user interface that we had in mind. By integrating actuators (vibrating motors and LEDs) with sensors (push buttons) into self-contained elements, we get a setting that intuitively bridges input and output, streamlining user experience.

We utilized an existing model of a push button cap from \textit{Printables}\cite{tactile_switch_cap_12x12} in order to get the right dimensions for the base connecting to the button. Next a bigger cap, accommodating the dimensions of LEDs and vibrating motors was constructed on top of it. Actuators were placed into the print midway and sealed completely, leaving only the connector points protruding from the sides.

Prototyping process took two iterations. For the final version we introduced smoother curves, consistent sizing between the two button types and picked flexible TPU filament as a material of choice to further improve tactile user experience.


\paragraph{Finger Rests}

These modules serve the purpose of holding push-buttons with custom key caps in place. They are embedded into the surface of the box enclosure and work together with its geometry, using it as palm rests.

Through the introduction of grooves accommodating fingers' shape and placement of the holes for button key caps we aimed at an ergonomic design, that will support an intuitive use of the product.


\paragraph{Players' ``Avatars''}

We opted for a ``space'' theme as a graphic aesthetic for the final product. This lead to the idea of choosing UFO flying saucer figurines as players' avatars. Yet again we borrowed a design from \textit{Printables}~\cite{alien_ufo_alext_2024}.

We modified it by scaling it down and introducing a cutout at the base of the saucer's beam, to accommodate the status indication RGB LED.


\subsubsection{Enclosure}

All of the components are enclosed into a $400mm \times 300mm \times 80mm$ box.

We used \textit{MakerCase}~\cite{makercase_basicBox} website to generate design that is easy to assemble thanks to box finger joints. Design was altered in Fusion 360 to introduce cutouts in the top lid, where 3D-printed modules holding the buttons and status indication RGB LEDs were to be placed.

First iteration of the box was cut out from cardboard for fast and cheap prototype and assembled with the help of duct tape. We test-fit the components and introduced necessary adjustments.

Second iteration featured smaller finger finger joints for easier assembly and cutouts for the power switch and charging port. It was also the final version, which we cut out with laser from a 6mm MDF board.

The prototype is split into two power rails. One of these is solely for the vibration motors, which operate properly in a relatively
narrow voltage range. In addition to this, we also have a separate power source\footnote{LiPo battery} for the motors, to avoid electrical interference,
which could negatively impact the microcontrollers.

The other power rail is directly connected to another LiPo battery, since the compoenents connected to this have a much wider 
operating voltage range. This means we avoid the efficiency loss from doing a voltage step-up. 
This means our power rails are, respectively, 3.4 volts\footnote{See operating voltage of vibration motors.} 
and 3.7 volts\footnote{Actually a voltage range since it's connected directly to the battery.}.

\subsection{Components}
\subsubsection{LiPo battery}\label{sec_component_lipo}
As mentioned, we use two LiPo batteries each of which has a capacity of 820 mAh~\cite[][p.1]{lipodatasheet}. They have an operating voltage range between 2.75 and 
4.2V~\cite[][p.3]{lipodatasheet}, at respectively fully discharged and charged states. They are also very reliable and safe~\cite[][p.4-6]{lipodatasheet}, which makes them well suited for a toy.

\subsubsection{ATmega328P}\label{sec_component_atmega}
The ATmega328P is used in our two agent boards, and control the actuators of the game. They also send information about button presses back to the controller upon request.
We make use of eight of their I/O pins directly for the buttons and actuators, and we also make use of their I\textsuperscript{2}C~\cite[][p.173]{atmega328p} capabilities to communicate with the
controller board. These work in a wide voltage range and have a fairly low power draw. 

Due to the simplicity of what we're using them for, and the reduced power draw, we've decided against running these at 16MHz using external crystals. This allows us to 
avoid stepping up the voltage, since running the chip at 8MHz is stable within the voltage range of a standard LiPo battery as seen in figure~\ref{fig:safe_operating_range}.
\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  % Reduced width from \linewidth to 0.7\linewidth per request
  \includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{figure/figure_4_1.png}
  \caption{Safe operating frequencies of the ATmega328P. Figure from~\cite[][p.260]{atmega328p}.}
  \label{fig:safe_operating_range}
\end{figure}

To be more specific, we can support voltages between 2.7 and 5.5 volts~\cite[][p.260]{atmega328p}, which is well within the range of the 3.7 volt LiPo battery. At the voltage we expect the microcontrollers
to operate at for the majority of the time, their power draw should each be about 3.1mA according to the datasheet~\cite[][p.268]{atmega328p}. 

A possible alternative to the atmega328p, could be a PIC line chip, like the PIC16F15244 or similar~\cite{atmegaalternative}. Like the atmega, these have a low power draw 
and work in a wide voltage range. They also support I\textsuperscript{2}C, which is a key part of our prototype. They also have a low power mode, where they are able to run
at very low clock frequencies, but in turn use even less than 1mA. This might be usable for this prototype, since the agents only need to react much faster than a human.
They aren't actually doing any heavy computation of any kind.

\subsubsection{ESP 32}\label{sec_component_esp32}
We use the ESP32-C3 super mini in our prototype as the controller of the game. The mictrocontroller requests information from the ATmega328P microcontrollers using 
I\textsuperscript{2}C~\cite[][p.4]{esp32}, and sends out appropriate commands. We use the hardware randomness module~\cite[][p.5]{esp32} within the ESP32, 
to generate sequences for the game. This ensures a reliable and unpredictable pattern. 
While the operating voltage of the ESP32 itself is only 3.0 to 3.6~\cite[][p.53]{esp32}, 
the module we're using has a power input pin that ranges from 3.3 to 6.0 volts~\cite[][p.3]{esp32supermini}.
Finding an exact expected power draw in the datasheet proved somewhat difficult due to the many possible configurations of the ESP32, but we have concluded it to be 
between 17 and 28mA~\cite[][p.56]{esp32}. 

An alternative to the ESP32, could be to just use an atmega328p or similar. We would need to source the randomness ourselves, but a noisy sensor could definitely do this. 
One advantage of the ESP32 is that, for future work, we could integrate the wireless capabilities into the prototype with little to no extra components. 
It could, for example, be possible to control the difficulty or keep a scoreboard from your phone. Maybe even connect multiple game boards and play as 4 players.

\subsubsection{Vibrating Motor}\label{sec_component_vibratingmotor}
The vibrating motors are used for tactile instructions for the player. They operate at between 2.5 and 3.8 volts~\cite[][p.5]{vibratingmotor},
which might lead one to think it could be run directly using the LiPo battery. However, the LiPo batteries actually reach up to 4.2 volts when fully charged, 
as mentioned in section~\ref{sec_component_lipo}, which would go over the limit of 3.8 volts. We therefore decided to step this down to 3.4 volts. 
The reason for this specific voltage will be explained in section~\ref{sec_component_ldo}. The motor has a maximum rated current draw of 75mA~\cite[][p.5]{vibratingmotor}.

An alternative motor could be the FIT0774~\cite{motoralternative}. It's a similar cheap vibrating motor, but it has a lower current limit. It does, however, have a 
wider voltage operating range, which goes up to the 4.2V like the batteries. This means it would be possible to avoid stepping down the voltage entirely. This would, however,
yield different vibration strengths depending on the current charge of the battery, which we have tried to avoid, as much as possible, by using an LDO regulator.

\subsubsection{Bipolar junction transistor}\label{sec_component_bjt}
To supply enough current to the vibrating motors, we can't just draw from a pin, since those are only rated for about 40mA~\cite[][p.258]{atmega328p}. 
We want more current than this, so we use the BC547~\cite{transistor}, which has a maximum collector current of 100mA~\cite[][p.1]{transistor}. 
This means our vibrating motors fit nicely within the current limit. 
The voltage rating is also more than sufficient, with both the collector-base and collector-emitter voltage rating being over 40 volts~\cite[][p.1]{transistor}. 
The emitter-base voltage rating is 6 volts~\cite[][p.1]{transistor}, which is well above our 4.2 maximum voltage.

\subsubsection{Light Emitting Diode}\label{sec_component_led}
For the LEDs we simply used the ones at the lab, so we don't have the exact datasheet. This isn't strictly necessary though, as LEDs of this size are somewhat standard.
We use both the standard single color LEDs and the multicolor LEDs. For the multicolor LEDs, we mostly found that they have a current rating of 20mA~\cite[][p.1]{multicolorled} 
per color. The forward voltage depends on the color, but for our usage, it should be respectively 2.3V and 3.4V for the red and green colors~\cite[][p.1]{multicolorled}. 

For the single color LED, we've used a standard datasheet we found on a reputable vendor site~\cite{led}, and concluded the rated current to be, at most, 30mA~\cite[][p.2]{led}, 
but we'll be a bit cautious and say 20mA, as that was the most common lower bound we could find for this type of LED. For the forward voltage it, again, depends on color, 
but we've chosen to assume a forward voltage of 2V\footnote{This should be below the correct value. }, as that will give a greater degree of caution than assuming a higher forward voltage. 
In practice, we've found the LEDs to be bright enough with these assumptions.

\subsubsection{Low Dropout Regulator}\label{sec_component_ldo}
To step down the voltage from the LiPo to 3.4 volts, we use a low dropout regulator~\cite{ldo}. These are a type of linear voltage regulator, with the advantage that they work 
even when the input voltage is close to the configured output voltage. This is exactly our use-case. Specifically, we're using the MIC29302WT, which has an output voltage 
range between 1.25V and 25V, and an input voltage range between 2.3V to 26V~\cite[][p.4-7]{ldo}\footnote{See note 7 in~\cite[][p.7]{ldo}.}. The rated current is up to 3A, 
which is way more than needed for this project, but since the component was bought by a person in the group, 
they wanted the component to be very versatile for other future personal projects, which is also the reason for the adjustable output voltage. 
A more suited component for this prototype could've been something like the R1517J341B-T1-FE~\cite{ldoalternative}, 
which has a lower current rating of 500mA and outputs 3.4 volts. 
\\
The reason for choosing 3.4 volt over 3.3 volt, was to keep the vibration strength at a higher level for longer. While the LDO can maintain the voltage, it does still have dropout. According
to the datasheet, for example, the output voltage might drop as low as 3V, when the battery has reached 3.5 volts, given a high enough current~\cite[][p.12]{ldo}. 
Though the current won't be that high in our case. To ensure proper vibration, we've therefore set the target voltage a bit higher than we actually need, 
to hopefully ensure a better player experience for a wider voltage range. \\[0.5pt]

\noindent The efficiency of the LDO is simply the output voltage divided by the input voltage, since it's a type of linear voltage regulator. This gives us an efficiency of about 
$\frac{3.4}{4.2} \approx 0.81$ in the worst case\footnote{The worst case efficiency is a fully charged battery. }.

All three PCBs have been secured from the sides against the bottom of the inside of the box with pieces of MDF.

On/Off switch was placed into one of the box-sides, together with the USB-C port for charging.

We positioned all of the finger rests modules into their respective cutouts in the top lid of the box and connected pairs of wires for all of the push-buttons, LEDs and vibration motors to the PCBs via JST connectors.


\subsection{Firmware}

Our group is made up of 3 Computer Science students - and therefore a clean and well-managed codebase was important to us.

The logic of the game consists of 3 phases:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Controller sends agents one-by-one the IDs to activate, then (after short pause) deactivate
  \item Controller requests button presses from player agents, checks if correct, sends feedback in a loop
  \begin{itemize}
    \item if incorrect, go to (3)
    \item if correct for all elements of pattern, switch to other player, go to (1)
  \end{itemize}
  \item Win/lose state, controller instructs agents on feedback
\end{enumerate}

The two ATmega328P agents are responsible for turning the components on and off when instructed by the controller. In Phase 2, they listen for button presses, and when requested by the controller, send back which button was pressed last. (They listen only for the rising edge of the button press signal, to avoid multiple readings of the same press.)\\
The agents share almost all of their code: \texttt{shared.h} contains the code for receiving commands from controller, and turning components off/on. The only difference between the two agent sketches is their own I\textsuperscript{2}C address and which specific pins are connected to which component/button - these are stored in the \texttt{I2C\_SELF\_ADDRESS}, and \texttt{nodes} constants respectively. \\
Setting up the Arduino IDE to allow this sharing of code between them was rather cumbersome, as different sketches cannot easily share a header file unless it is defined as a library on a user level. This would have made source control complicated. The solution we found instead, was to have the header file symlinked to both agent sketches. The link is not tracked by version control - only the original header file in a directory of its own outside the 2 sketches.

The controller is the ESP32: this handles all logic on the scale of individually addressing components to turn them on and off. It works with global IDs assigned to actuator–button pairs, and does not know specific Atmega pins, only which agent a given ID belongs to.\\
In our initial design, both players would have had 2 agents, but we ended up scaling back from this. However, by then we have already set up broadcasting, which would have let the controller not keep track of which agent is which, just which agent belongs to which player. The broadcasting functionality is still present in the codebase and still used in the feedback, where both status LEDs are instructed to stop blinking at the same time.

The game logic is set up as a state machine, illustrated in figure~\ref{fig:state_machine}. The game states are separate from the feedback states, which are used to control the status LEDs. This separation allows for non-blocking feedback - for example, the status LEDs can blink while the game is waiting for user input. This also requires never using \texttt{delay()} in the code, instead relying on \texttt{millis()} to keep track of time.

\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{figure/state_machine.png}
  \caption{State machine diagram of the game logic, created with PlantUML.}
  \label{fig:state_machine}
\end{figure}


The gameplay consists of ``turns'' (one pattern then corresponding feedback for one player) of increasing ``difficulty''. Difficulty increases after each ``round'' (after each player had a turn), so the two turns are the same difficulty. (Of course some patterns of the same difficulty according to our generator may feel easier or harder - this will always be the case.)\\
The time taken for each turn does not increase much, but increasing difficulty does:
\begin{itemize}
  \item shorten the time for which an actuator is activated
  \item shorten the time between different actuator activations
  \item increase the number of elements in a pattern
\end{itemize}

Given more time, we could run experiments with users to find the difficulty scaling curve that feels best. The current function ``feels good'' to us, but it's not backed up by any evidence.

On another note, using the ESP32 gave us the possibility of wireless debugging! The ESP runs a simple web server that serves a page with Serial out. This uses the ESP's WiFi capabilities and the MycilaWebSerial lib. The whole thing can be disabled at compile time via a \texttt{\#define}, to avoid the extra power draw.
This proved very useful during development, as we could see Serial output without needing to connect the ESP to a computer via USB. The WiFi capabilities could also be a basis of future work, as mentioned in section~\ref{sec_component_esp32}.


\subsection{Electronics Calculations}
\subsubsection{Sequence LEDs}
As mentioned in section~\ref{sec_component_led}, we'll assume a forward voltage of 2V, and we'll target a current at about 20mA. The LEDs are on the 3.7V power rail.
To calculate the resistance of the needed resistor, we'll first calulate the voltage across the resistor as $V_R=V_{supply}-V_F=1.7\text{V}$. After this it's a simple
calculation using ohm's law with
\[
	R=\frac{V_R}{I}=\frac{1.7\text{V}}{20\text{mA}}=85\Omega
\]
We'll then round this up to a standard resistor value to ensure that we don't exceed the current limit we set. Which is why resistors 
R10 and R11 in figure~\ref{fig:agent_board_schematic} are 100 ohm resistors.

\subsubsection{Multicolor LEDs}
The calculations for these LEDs look very similar. You can see the values in section~\ref{sec_component_led}. The calculated values are as follows:
\[
	R_{red}=\frac{V_{supply}-V_F}{I}=\frac{1.4\text{V}}{20\text{mA}}=70\Omega
\]
\[
	R_{green}=\frac{V_{supply}-V_F}{I}=\frac{0.3\text{V}}{20\text{mA}}=15\Omega
\]
For safety, we round these up to respectively 100 ohms and 33 ohms, for respectively resistors R15 and R14 in figure~\ref{fig:agent_board_schematic}.

\subsubsection{Motors}
For the resistor limiting the current to the motor itself, it's once again just ohms law\footnote{See section~\ref{sec_component_vibratingmotor} for motor values}.
This gives us
\[
	R=\frac{V_{supply}}{I}=\frac{3.4}{75\text{mA}}=45.\overline{3}\Omega
\]
Which we round up to 47 ohms\footnote{This value also works if we calculate with an extra bit of safety using 3.5V instead of 3.4V.}. This is the value of resistors
R12 and R13 in figure~\ref{fig:agent_board_schematic}.

\subsubsection{Transistors}
Since we're using the transistors as switches, we'll be operating them in forced beta condition. Specifically we'll be using a forced beta of 10, meaning
the base current will be a tenth of the desired collector current. This will ensure current can flow correctly through the collector at the rate we want.
Some simple math tells us that this means the base current will be $I_B=\frac{I_C}{\beta}=\frac{75\text{mA}}{10}=7.5\text{mA}$. To calculate the resistance value
needed, we then use the saturation voltage of the base-emitter~\cite[][p.1]{transistor}, which finally gives us the calculation
\[
	R=\frac{V_{supply}-V_{BE_{sat}}}{I_B}=\frac{2.6\text{V}}{7.5\text{mA}}=373.\overline{3}\Omega
\]
For this value, we'll round down to ensure that the base is saturated. Thus we've chosen a resistor value of 330 ohms for resistors
R6 and R8 in figure~\ref{fig:agent_board_schematic}.

\subsubsection{Low dropout regulator}
Since we're using an adjustable LDO, we'll need to set the voltage using a voltage divider. The MIC29302WT has an internal reference voltage of 1.240V~\cite[][p.6]{ldo}, which is 
used in collaboration with the adjustment pin to set the voltage. The voltage divider needs two resistors, and the resistance values of these is calculated using the equation
in the datasheet~\cite[][p.25]{ldo}. We'll need to choose one of the resistor values and then calculate the other. Using the examples in the datasheet~\cite[][p.3]{ldo}, we've chosen 
R2 to have a resistance of 390 ohms. We can then calculate the value of R1 using~\cite[][p.25]{ldo}
\[
	R1=R2\left(\frac{V_{out}}{1.24}-1\right)=390\left(\frac{3.4}{1.24}-1\right)\approx 679
\]
which is almost exactly 680 ohms. We've therefore chosen this resistor value. The resistors in the circuit diagrams are R1 and R2 in figure~\ref{fig:controller_board_schematic}, 
with the same names as in the calculations. Note that the datasheet also mentions a minimum load to have the correct output voltage~\cite[][p.25]{ldo}. 
This is due to current leakage. We don't actually have a constant load on the output 
beside the voltage divider, which doesn't draw enough current. We've tested the voltage in practice, and found that the leakage is so small, that the motors will be 
completely fine\footnote{The voltage went up to about 3.55V, but since motors use inductors to work, this will limit the current for the short period of time it takes the
voltage to stabilize to the correct value. In addition, the motor resistor is actually also almost the correct value even with 3.55V.}. 

For the capacitors used, we simply used the values from the datasheet~\cite[][p.26]{ldo}. This gives us the values for capacitors 
C1 and C2 in figure~\ref{fig:controller_board_schematic}, which are 22$\mu$F and 10$\mu$F. 

\subsubsection{The rest of the resistors}
The rest of the resistors are either pull-up or pull-down resistors in the 1K-22K range. The specific values weren't calculated as such, but were instead chosen either 
based on examples in the related datasheet or based on values used in lectures given. The specific value shouldn't be too important, unless a specific component has a 
current limit on the pin where the resistor is located. This is not the case for the resistors we chose ourselves, as these were pull-downs for buttons and switches.

\section{Limitations and future work}
%In the limitations section, identify constraints or drawbacks of your prototype. These could include technical limitations, cost restrictions, or any design choices that may have affected the prototype's performance. Be honest about the areas where improvements or refinements could be made. The future work could outline potential enhancements and extensions for your prototype. One very obvious limitation of the prototype is the manufacturing method. The PCBs could be much more compact and reliable 
given more modern manufacturing methods. The components themselves could also be smaller, since modern pick and place assembly lines 
can use SMD components instead of the through-hole components we use for the prototype. The cost-effectiveness of this would,
however, heavily depend on the scale of product manufacturing. In addition to these points, using ribbon connectors or some similar 
connector made for the purpose of data transfer and power transfer between the boards would also improve reliability. 

As for the design of the external part of the prototype, we have considered adding one more button for each modality, for a total of six buttons 
per player. We also wanted to add a sound module to add to the experience using jingles and maybe some audio cues. We did also consider making sound 
an aspect of the game, but it's a bit unclear at this point, how that might work.

The 3D printed finger rests themselves feel really nice in our opinion, but when the buttons are added to the motor finger rests, it 
does interfere a bit in an uncomfortable way, which we would want to fix in another iteration of the button design.

For the purposes of the prototype, the box assembly has been great, but we would also want to add hand rests for comfort, and some more decorations
to enhance the visual appeal of the game. We think the choice of making the box out of wood is perfectly fine though, since the game 
is meant as an indoor experience, but we might want to look into other methods of manufacturing for more control over the 
surface geometry of the box for the afformentioned changes.
